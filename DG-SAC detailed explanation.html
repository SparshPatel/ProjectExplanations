One-Minute Overview

Delta hedging degrades under real market conditions: stochastic volatility, jumps, and non-negligible transaction costs. RL agents can adapt, but suffer from poor sample efficiency and unstable cross-model transfer. DG-SAC resolves both by embedding the Black–Scholes delta as a decaying inductive bias in the reward.

Problem

Two Practical Challenges

Sample efficiency: model-free RL must explore from scratch, ignoring that hedging near the analytical delta is a reasonable starting point. Robustness: agents trained under GBM produce erratic ratios under rough volatility or jump-diffusion dynamics.

Approach

Time-Decaying Delta Guidance

The shaped reward is $r^\text{DG}(s_t,a_t) = r(s_t,a_t) - \beta(t)(a_t - \Delta^\text{BS}_t)^2$, where $\beta(t) = \beta_0(1-\eta\,t/T)$ decays from strong early guidance to weak late-stage regularisation. No architectural changes to SAC are required.

Validation

1 · The Problem

The Black–Scholes model computes the hedge ratio by differentiating the pricing formula with respect to the underlying price. While elegant in a frictionless world, it degrades under the conditions that characterise real markets.

Failure 1

Model Assumptions Violated

Black–Scholes assumes geometric Brownian motion, constant volatility, and frictionless continuous trading. Real markets exhibit stochastic and path-dependent volatility, jumps, and non-negligible bid–ask spreads — all of which make the analytical delta systematically wrong.

Failure 2

RL: Poor Sample Efficiency

Model-free RL requires extensive exploration to discover that hedging near the analytical delta is a reasonable starting point — even when the trader's prior model is approximately correct. This wastes training budget on rediscovering known structure.

Failure 3

RL: Cross-Model Fragility

Agents trained under GBM may produce erratic hedge ratios when deployed under a different data-generating process such as rough volatility or jump-diffusion dynamics. There is no built-in mechanism to transfer structural knowledge across model classes.

Merton Jump-Diffusion: Why Delta Fails

Merton (1976) extended GBM with Poisson-driven jumps. The Black–Scholes delta is systematically wrong under Merton dynamics because it ignores jump risk. The compensator $k = e^{\mu_J + \sigma_J^2/2} - 1$ ensures $\mathbb{E}[dS/S] = \mu\,dt$, but delta hedging remains incomplete.

Merton jump-diffusion:

$$dS/S = (\mu - \lambda k)\,dt + \sigma\,dW + J\,dN$$

$\lambda = 1.0$ (≈ one jump per year), $\mu_J = -0.05$ (average downward jumps), $\sigma_J = 0.10$. Produces fat tails and an implied volatility smile without stochastic volatility.

Rough Bergomi: Non-Markovian Volatility

$$dS/S = \mu\,dt + \sqrt{V_t}\,dW^1, \quad \text{Hurst parameter } H = 0.07$$

The fractional Brownian motion driver with $H = 0.07$ produces non-Markovian, path-dependent volatility dynamics that invalidate the delta hedging derivation entirely. This is the most challenging test environment.

2 · DG-SAC: The Method

DG-SAC augments the SAC reward with a time-decaying delta-guidance penalty. The design is a curriculum: the agent is most constrained early (when it knows least and the analytical delta is most informative) and most autonomous late.

MDP Formulation

State space — 8-dimensional feature vector:

$$s_t = \bigl(S_t/S_0,\; \tau_t,\; \Delta^\text{BS}_t,\; \Gamma^\text{BS}_t S_t,\; V^\text{BS}_t,\; h_t,\; \text{PnL}_t/C_0,\; \sigma_t\bigr) \in \mathbb{R}^8$$

$\tau_t = (T-t)/T \in [0,1]$ is remaining time fraction, $h_t \in [0,1]$ is the current hedge position, $\text{PnL}_t$ is cumulative P&L normalised by initial option price $C_0$.

Action space: $a_t \in [0,1]$ — the new hedge ratio (fraction of one unit of the underlying held).

Base reward:

$$r(s_t, a_t) = -\left(\frac{\text{PnL}_t}{C_0}\right)^2 - \lambda_{tc}\frac{c_t}{C_0}$$

$c_t = \lambda_{tc} \cdot tc \cdot S_t \cdot |a_t - h_{t-1}|$, where $tc$ is the proportional transaction cost rate.

The Shaped Reward

DG-SAC reward:

$$r^\text{DG}(s_t, a_t) = r(s_t, a_t) - \beta(t)\left(a_t - \Delta^\text{BS}_t\right)^2$$

Concave decay schedule:

$$\beta(t) = \beta_0\!\left(1 - \eta \cdot \frac{t}{T}\right), \quad \beta_0 = 0.8,\;\; \eta = 0.8$$

$\beta(0) = 0.8$ (strong guidance at episode start), $\beta(T) = 0.16$ (weak but non-zero regularisation at expiry). The residual guidance at expiry prevents degenerate policies near maturity. The guidance is additive to the reward — the standard SAC soft Bellman backup applies without modification.

Key design insight: The Black–Scholes delta, while imperfect under misspecification, provides a strong initialisation prior that accelerates convergence and stabilises cross-model transfer. Because the guidance is additive to the reward rather than a hard constraint, no changes to SAC's convergence machinery are needed.

Three Structural Properties (by Construction)

Proposition 1 — SAC Recovery: Setting $\beta_0 = 0$ recovers standard SAC exactly: $r^\text{DG}(s_t,a_t)\big|_{\beta_0=0} = r(s_t,a_t)$. DG-SAC is a strict generalisation.

Proposition 2 — Bounded Perturbation: $\bigl|r^\text{DG}(s_t,a_t) - r(s_t,a_t)\bigr| \le \beta_0$ for all $(s,a,t)$. The guidance cannot catastrophically distort the reward landscape.

Proposition 3 — Monotone Decay: $\beta(t+1) < \beta(t)$ for all $t \in \{0,\ldots,T-2\}$ when $\eta > 0$. Guidance strength strictly decreases throughout each episode.

3 · Theoretical Analysis

Two theorems characterise DG-SAC's guarantees: asymptotic optimality (Theorem 4) and a finite-time regret decomposition (Theorem 7). Both are proved in the paper; their key implications are shown here.

Theorem 4: Asymptotic Optimality

Let $\pi^*$ be the optimal SAC policy and $\pi^\text{DG}_\theta$ the DG-SAC policy. As gradient updates $N \to \infty$:

$$J_\text{SAC}(\pi^\text{DG}_\theta) \ge J_\text{SAC}(\pi^*) - \beta_0 T \cdot \sup_{s,t} \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi^*}\!\left[(a - \Delta^\text{BS}_t)^2 \,\big|\, s\right]$$

Corollary 5: If $\pi^*$ is close to the BS delta (deviation $\le \varepsilon$), the guidance bias is $O(\beta_0 T \varepsilon)$, vanishing as $\beta_0 \to 0$ or as the optimal policy aligns with BS-delta.

Corollary 6 (Zero-Cost Guidance): When the BS model is correctly specified and $\pi^* = \Delta^\text{BS}$, the guidance bias vanishes exactly: $J_\text{SAC}(\pi^\text{DG}_\theta) \ge J_\text{SAC}(\pi^*)$.

Empirical validation: Empirical guidance bias = 0.0063 vs. theoretical upper bound = 0.1040. Tightness ratio = 0.061 — the bound holds with a 16.5× margin, confirming the worst-case analysis is conservative.

Theorem 7: Finite-Time Regret Decomposition

Regret relative to the clairvoyant oracle decomposes as:

$$\text{Regret}_N \le \underbrace{C_1\sqrt{N \log N}}_{\text{estimation / exploration}} + \underbrace{C_2\bar\varepsilon T \sum_{n=1}^N \bar\beta_n}_{\text{guidance bias}} + \underbrace{C_3 \cdot N \,\varepsilon_\text{approx}}_{\text{approx. error}}$$

$\bar\beta_n = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\beta_n(t)$ is mean guidance strength at episode $n$. The replay buffer size $B$ enters only through $C_1$ (reducing gradient variance) — it does not affect the guidance-bias term.

Asymptotic rate: Sublinear $O(\sqrt{N\log N})$ regret requires annealing guidance across training ($\bar\beta_n \to 0$). The fixed within-episode concave schedule contributes a small linear term — manifesting as faster early convergence rather than an improved asymptotic rate. This is exactly the behaviour observed empirically.

7 · Discussion & Limitations

Why Delta Guidance Works

Mechanism 1

Warm Start in Action Space

The delta prior places the agent's initial actions in a high-reward region, bypassing the random exploration phase that dominates early SAC training. Particularly valuable when the action space is continuous and the reward landscape is sparse early in training.

Mechanism 2

Variance Reduction

By anchoring early actions to BS-delta, DG-SAC reduces the variance of Q-value estimates in the replay buffer, leading to more stable critic updates and more reliable policy gradients.

Mechanism 3

Graceful Degradation Under Misspecification

When the BS model is misspecified (Merton, rough Bergomi), BS-delta remains a reasonable first approximation. The decaying $\beta$ allows the agent to learn corrections while retaining the structural benefit of starting near a known good solution.

Mechanism 4

Seed Robustness & Implicit Regularisation

DG-SAC's confidence intervals are 2–6× narrower than SAC's — the delta prior shields the agent from poor random initialisations. The residual guidance at expiry ($\beta(T) = 0.16 > 0$) also acts as a soft regulariser preventing overfitting to training noise.

Key Finding: Merton — Environment-Specific Training Adds Little

Training directly on Merton dynamics provides only a 4% improvement over GBM-pretrained DG-SAC (HE ≈ 0.048 vs. ≈ 0.050). The delta prior — not the training environment — is the primary driver of robustness. Reward shaping and architecture matter more than environment fidelity.

Ablation: Decay Schedule

Four schedules were tested: (i) constant ($\beta = 0.5$), (ii) linear ($0.8 \to 0$), (iii) concave ($0.8 \to 0.16$, default), (iv) exponential ($0.8 \cdot e^{-3t/T}$). The concave schedule with residual guidance achieves the best hedging error–transaction cost trade-off.

Limitations (Fully Reported)

Limitation 1

Delta Dominates at Low Friction

Under correctly specified GBM with tc ≤ 0.3%, delta hedging outperforms all RL agents by ≈17%. RL is complementary to, not a replacement for, analytical methods. A hybrid controller switching by friction regime is a natural next step.

Limitation 2

Portfolio Scalability Gap

RL agents are 2.2× worse than analytical delta for a 4-leg portfolio. Scaling to $M \gg 4$ legs likely requires attention-based architectures, 1M+ training steps, and learned action correlation structures.

Limitation 3

Heterogeneous Fine-Tuning

IWM improvement (3%) is far below SPY (28%) and QQQ (26%), likely from lower liquidity and shorter history. Minimum data quality and quantity for effective transfer learning in options markets remains an open question.

Limitation 4

Limited Multi-Seed Power

Five seeds yield $p = 0.062$ on rough Bergomi — insufficient for high statistical power. Determining sample sizes for reliable evaluation under non-Markovian volatility deserves further study.

Limitation 5

Hyperparameter Sensitivity

$\beta_0 = 0.8$ and $\eta = 0.8$ were set via domain intuition and ablation, not systematic optimisation. Different market conditions may benefit from different schedules. Meta-learning the decay schedule online is a promising direction.

Limitation 6

Backtest Limitations

Real options backtests use calibrated implied volatility and simulated transaction costs — not actual execution. Only live trading can validate the fine-tuning gains reported here. Quantifying the sim-to-real gap for RL hedging agents remains to be done.

Suggested Extensions

Extension 1

Bayesian Schedule Optimisation

Systematic Bayesian optimisation of the $\beta$ decay schedule, rather than the domain-intuition setting used here, to find globally optimal guidance parameters across friction regimes.

Extension 2

High-Dimensional Portfolios

Attention-based architectures and factorised action spaces for portfolio hedging with $M \gg 4$ legs — necessary to make RL competitive with analytical delta in high-dimensional settings.

Extension 3

Path-Dependent Options

Extension to barrier options and Asian options, where analytical deltas are themselves approximations — a setting where DG-SAC's misspecification robustness may be most valuable.
