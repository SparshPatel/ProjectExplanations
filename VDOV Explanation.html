01 Abstract

This paper investigates volatility-managed portfolios over what is, to our knowledge, the longest daily sample studied in this context: 100 years of returns from the Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP) database (1926-2025; 26,190 trading days). Working within a Heston (1993) stochastic volatility framework combined with Merton (1971) portfolio choice, we show that the expected Sharpe ratio gain from volatility targeting satisfies:

$$\Delta SR \propto SR_0 \times cv(\sigma)^2$$

where SR_0 denotes the asset's base Sharpe ratio and cv(sigma) its volatility's coefficient of variation. Building on this result, we develop a Kelly-type allocation rule: Variance-Drain-Optimal Volatility management (VDOV), and validate it through a strict walk-forward protocol. VDOV delivers an out-of-sample Sharpe ratio of 1.01, compared with 0.94 for the Moreira and Muir (2017) volatility target and 0.79 for buy-and-hold.

We also uncover a structural break near 1985: before that date, volatility scaling was on average harmful (ΔSR = −0.047), but afterward it became reliably beneficial (ΔSR = +0.058; p = 0.014), a shift we attribute to the growth of derivatives markets and financial deregulation. Panel regressions on 1,067 asset-window observations across 21 assets confirm that both the variance drain channel (t = 7.09) and the leverage effect (t = 18.60) independently predict Sharpe ratio improvements.

International tests reinforce these conclusions: VDOV outperforms both benchmarks out of sample in four of five Kenneth French regional markets (80%).

02 Introduction

The Volatility Targeting Debate

How should an investor respond when market volatility spikes? Moreira and Muir (2017) proposed a disarmingly simple answer: scale your equity allocation by the inverse of recent realized variance. On US stock data, this "volatility-managed portfolio" delivered a meaningfully higher Sharpe ratio than buy-and-hold, and the result extended to bonds, credit, and currency carry.

The pushback, though, has been sharp. Cederburg et al. (2020) ran the same exercise through a Bayesian lens and found that, once parameter uncertainty is taken seriously, most of the apparent Sharpe improvement washes out in out-of-sample tests. Barroso and Detzel (2021) pointed to a more mundane problem: the strategy trades frequently, and at realistic bid-ask spreads, a large chunk of the alpha disappears. Liu et al. (2019) added a further qualification, showing that the gains are concentrated in high-volatility episodes and largely absent, or even negative during quiet markets.

Our Contribution: Going Long

We try to make progress on this debate by going long, in the temporal sense. Specifically, we assemble a century of daily equity returns from the CRSP database via the Kenneth French Data Library, running from July 1926 through February 2026: 26,190 trading days spanning the Great Depression, World War II, the Bretton Woods era, the Great Moderation, the 2008 Global Financial Crisis, and the COVID-19 pandemic.

This sample is roughly four times the length of the post-1963 window that underpins most prior studies, and it affords considerably more statistical power to detect, or reject, the patterns that interest us.

Four Key Contributions

1

Theoretical: The ΔSR Formula

Embedding the Moreira-Muir strategy within a continuous-time Heston-Merton framework, we derive a closed-form expression: ΔSR ∝ SR_0 × cv(σ)². This reveals why the cruder approximation ΔSR ≈ ½cv(σ)², which omits SR_0 entirely, can mislead.

2

Methodological: VDOV

We develop Variance-Drain-Optimal Volatility management (VDOV), a rule that blends a fractional-Kelly allocation with a standard volatility target. Under strict expanding-window walk-forward tests across eight non-overlapping decades (1950-2025), VDOV achieves an aggregate OOS Sharpe ratio of 1.01, outpacing M&M (0.94) and buy-and-hold (0.79).

3

Empirical: The 1985 Structural Break

We uncover a structural break around 1985. Before that date, volatility scaling was on average harmful (ΔSR = −0.047); afterward, it became reliably beneficial (ΔSR = +0.058; p = 0.014). The break coincides with a measurable rise in cv(σ) from 0.44 to 0.52, traced to financial deregulation and derivatives market expansion.

4

Mechanistic: Two Channels

Panel regressions on 1,067 asset-window observations confirm that both the variance drain channel (t = 7.09) and the leverage effect (t = 18.60) carry independent explanatory power, though the leverage channel dominates (R² rises from 0.08 to 0.42 when included).

An Intriguing By-Product: Simpson's Paradox

An intriguing by-product of the analysis is a Simpson's paradox: vol-targeting lowers the Sharpe ratio within every volatility tercile, yet raises it unconditionally, exactly the pattern our formula predicts when cv(σ) is small within regimes but large across them.

Why this matters: The unconditional vol-targeted Sharpe ratio is SR_VT = (Σ p_k w_k μ_k) / √(Σ p_k w_k² σ_k²) where w_k = σ̄/σ_k. Since w_k is inversely proportional to σ_k, the portfolio overweights calm, high-SR regimes (w_Low > 1) and underweights turbulent, low-SR regimes (w_High < 1), producing SR_VT > SR_BH despite SR_VT_k < SR_BH_k within each regime.

03 Theoretical Framework

3.1 Setup: Portfolio Choice Under Stochastic Volatility

We begin with a stylized setting: an investor choosing between a single risky asset and a risk-free bond in continuous time. The risky asset's price evolves according to the Heston (1993) stochastic volatility model:

$$\frac{dS_t}{S_t} = \mu dt + \sqrt{v_t} \, dW_t^{(1)}$$ $$dv_t = \kappa(\bar{v} - v_t)dt + \xi\sqrt{v_t} \, dW_t^{(2)}$$

where S_t is the asset price, μ is the constant expected return, v_t = σ_t² is the instantaneous variance, κ > 0 is the speed of mean reversion, v̄ is the long-run mean variance, ξ > 0 is the volatility of variance ("vol-of-vol"), and W_t^(1) and W_t^(2) are standard Brownian motions with correlation Corr(dW_t^(1), dW_t^(2)) = ρ, where ρ < 0 captures the leverage effect.

The unconditional moments of the variance process are:

$$E[v_t] = \bar{v}, \quad Var(v_t) = \frac{\xi^2 \bar{v}}{2\kappa}, \quad cv(v_t) = \frac{\xi}{\sqrt{2\kappa \bar{v}}}$$

Since σ_t = √v_t, the coefficient of variation of volatility is related by cv(σ) ≈ ½ cv(v) for small fluctuations, connecting the Heston parameters directly to our testable prediction.

3.2 Proposition 1: Merton-Optimal Allocation

The investor maximizes expected utility of terminal wealth with CRRA coefficient γ > 1. The myopic component of the optimal allocation is:

$$w_t^* = \frac{\mu - r_f}{\gamma v_t}$$

The complete solution includes a hedging demand term h(v_t, ρ, ξ, κ) that depends on the investor's horizon and the covariance between the asset and its variance process. For typical calibrations (|ρ| < 0.7, ξ < 1), the hedging demand is small relative to the myopic component.

Key Insight

What matters for our purposes is the form of the myopic term: position size scales as 1/v_t, which means the Merton-optimal investor is, in effect, already a volatility targeter. The practical strategy studied by Moreira and Muir (2017) can thus be viewed as a simplified implementation of a theoretically optimal rule.

3.3 Proposition 2: Expected Sharpe Ratio Improvement

Under discrete compounding with time-varying volatility, the log return over a single period is:

$$\ln(1+r_t) = r_t - \frac{r_t^2}{2} + O(r_t^3) \approx r_t - \frac{\sigma_t^2}{2}$$

The annualized geometric return of a buy-and-hold portfolio is approximately:

$$r_{geo} \approx r_{arith} - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}$$

where σ²/2 is the variance drain ; the wedge between arithmetic and geometric returns. Decomposing the average variance when volatility is time-varying:

$$E[\sigma_t^2] = (E[\sigma_t])^2 + Var(\sigma_t) = \bar{\sigma}^2(1 + cv(\sigma)^2)$$

The variance drain for a buy-and-hold investor is VD_BH = σ̄²(1 + cv(σ)²)/2, while for a perfect volatility targeter (σ_t^scaled ≡ σ̄ for all t): VD_VT = σ̄²/2. The reduction in variance drain is:

$$\Delta VD = VD_{BH} - VD_{VT} = \frac{\bar{\sigma}^2 \cdot cv(\sigma)^2}{2}$$

Converting to Sharpe ratio units by dividing by σ̄, the expected Sharpe ratio improvement is:

$$\boxed{\Delta SR \propto SR_0 \times cv(\sigma)^2}$$

Intuition: cv(σ)² Term

Measures how much volatility varies over time. If volatility were constant (cv(σ) = 0), there would be nothing to scale, and the benefit would vanish.

Intuition: SR₀ Term

Captures the opportunity cost of time-varying exposure. An asset with a high Sharpe ratio suffers more from holding too little exposure during calm periods and too much during turbulent periods.

The Naive Approximation Trap

The simpler approximation ΔSR ≈ ½ cv(σ)², which appears occasionally in practitioner work, emerges as a special case when SR₀ is implicitly treated as constant and close to one. This can mislead when SR₀ differs substantially from 1 or varies across assets.

3.4 Proposition 3: Panel Prediction

Across assets i and time windows t, the Sharpe ratio improvement from volatility targeting satisfies:

$$\Delta SR_{i,t} = \alpha_i + \beta \cdot SR_{0,i,t} \times cv(\sigma_{i,t})^2 + \varepsilon_{i,t}$$

where α_i is an asset-specific fixed effect, SR_{0,i,t} is the base Sharpe ratio of asset i in window t, and cv(σ_{i,t}) is the coefficient of variation of volatility. Under the null, β ≈ 1. This panel specification exploits within-asset time-series variation, providing far more statistical power than a pure cross-section.

05 Main Empirical Results

5.1 Volatility Targeting on 100 Years of CRSP Data

Our baseline implementation follows Moreira and Muir (2017). At the close of each trading day t, we estimate current volatility using a 21-day exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA), which assigns geometrically declining weight to past squared returns. The EWMA variance is computed recursively:

$$\hat{v}_t^{EWMA} = (1-\lambda)r_t^2 + \lambda \hat{v}_{t-1}^{EWMA}$$

where λ = 1 − 2/(s + 1) is the decay factor and s = 21 is the span parameter, corresponding to a half-life of approximately 10 trading days. The annualized volatility estimate is σ̂_t = √(252 · v̂_t^EWMA). We compute the portfolio weight for day t+1 as:

$$w_{t+1} = \min\left\{ \frac{\bar{\sigma}}{\hat{\sigma}_t}, 2 \right\}$$

where σ̄ = 0.10 is a fixed annualized volatility target and the cap at 2 prevents excessive leverage. The vol-targeted return on day t+1 is:

$$r_{t+1}^{scaled} = w_{t+1} \cdot r_{t+1}^{mkt}$$

Full-Sample Results (1926-2025)

Over the full 1926-2025 sample, vol-targeting lifts the annualized Sharpe ratio from 0.63 to 0.84, an improvement of ΔSR = +0.21, and raises the geometric return from 9.77% to 12.22%.

06 VDOV: Variance-Drain-Optimal Volatility Management

6.1 Motivation and Construction

A pure volatility target, as in Moreira and Muir (2017), scales position size by 1/σ̂_t regardless of the prevailing risk premium. But Proposition 1 reminds us that the theoretically optimal weight, w_t^* = (μ − r_f)/(γ v_t), depends on expected returns too. Whenever expected returns vary over time ; and a large literature suggests they do (Campbell and Viceira, 1999) ; a fixed volatility target leaves money on the table.

The Kelly (1956) criterion provides the foundation for growth-optimal position sizing. For a discrete-time investor with log utility (γ = 1), the optimal fraction is:

$$w_t^{Kelly} = \frac{E_t[r_{t+1}]}{Var_t(r_{t+1})} = \frac{\hat{\mu}_t}{\hat{\sigma}_t^2}$$

For CRRA utility with γ > 1, the fractional Kelly allocation scales inversely with risk aversion:

$$w_t^{fKelly} = \frac{1}{\gamma} \cdot \frac{\hat{\mu}_t}{\hat{\sigma}_t^2} = \frac{\hat{\mu}_t}{\gamma \hat{\sigma}_t^2}$$

Of course, estimating expected returns is notoriously hard ; the signal-to-noise ratio scales as O(1/√T), making point estimates unreliable even with decades of data. We handle this by shrinking toward a long-run mean:

$$\hat{\mu}_t = (1-\lambda_t)\bar{\mu}_{grand} + \lambda_t \bar{\mu}_{1yr,t}, \quad \lambda_t = \min\left\{\frac{252}{N_t}, 1\right\}$$

where μ̄_grand is the expanding-window grand mean, μ̄_{1yr,t} is the trailing one-year mean, and N_t is the number of observations available through time t. Early in the sample (small N_t), the estimator shrinks heavily toward the grand mean; as data accumulate, it allows more weight on the recent mean.

The VDOV Portfolio Weight

$$w_t^{VDOV} = \text{clip}\left(0.6 \cdot w_t^{Kelly} + 0.4 \cdot w_t^{vol}, \, 0, \, 2\right)$$

where w_t^Kelly = μ̂_t / (γ σ̂_t²) and w_t^vol = σ̄_train / σ̂_t. The 60/40 blend between Kelly and vol-target components is not arbitrary: a grid search over w_Kelly ∈ [0,1] reveals a flat optimum spanning [0.4, 0.8], within which both in-sample and OOS Sharpe ratios remain within 5% of the peak.

6.2 Walk-Forward Validation Protocol

We implement a strict expanding-window walk-forward test on the 100-year CRSP sample:

1

For each decade d ∈ {1950, 1960, ..., 2020}, define the training period as 1926 through year d−1 and the test period as year d through year d+9.

2

On the training data, search over a grid of risk-aversion values γ ∈ [0.5, 3.0] and select the γ that maximizes the geometric return.

3

Apply the estimated γ and training-set parameters (mean volatility σ̄_train) to the test period. No information from the test period enters the parameter estimates.

4

Concatenate the eight OOS test periods and compute aggregate performance statistics.

The same training-window σ̄_train is applied to the M&M benchmark in each decade. Note that σ̄_train affects only the scale of positions, not the Sharpe ratio: since SR(k · r_t) = SR(r_t) for any scalar k > 0, the Sharpe ratio comparisons are invariant to this normalization choice.

07 Economic Mechanism: Panel Regression Evidence

7.1 Panel Construction

Why does vol-targeting improve the Sharpe ratio? Two channels have been proposed: variance drain reduction, which is the geometric-return benefit of stabilizing portfolio volatility, and the leverage effect, which allows the investor to exploit the negative correlation between returns and future volatility (Black, 1976; Christie, 1982).

Importantly, we interpret variance drain not as a market-level friction that deters arbitrage ; the compounding penalty on long positions flips sign for short sellers, so it cannot explain why overpriced stocks remain mispriced (Stambaugh et al., 2015) ; but as a portfolio-level cost that the representative long investor manages through volatility scaling.

To separate these channels empirically, we compute ΔSR, SR_0, cv(σ), and the leverage correlation ρ_{r,σ} for each of 21 assets over rolling three-year (756-day) windows, stepping by six months (126 days). This produces N = 1,067 asset-window observations.

$$\rho_{i,t} = \frac{\sum_{s \in T_t}(r_{i,s} - \bar{r}_i)(\hat{\sigma}_{i,s+1} - \bar{\hat{\sigma}}_i)}{\sqrt{\sum_s(r_{i,s} - \bar{r}_i)^2 \cdot \sum_s(\hat{\sigma}_{i,s+1} - \bar{\hat{\sigma}}_i)^2}}$$

The two competing predictors of ΔSR are the variance drain predictor VD_{i,t} = SR_{0,i,t} × cv(σ_{i,t})² and the leverage effect predictor ρ_{i,t} = Corr(r_s, σ_{s+1}) for s within window t.

Pattern 1: Variance Drain is Significant, but Leverage Dominates

The variance drain channel is significant on its own (t = 7.09), but the leverage effect is considerably more powerful (t = 18.60): adding ρ to the model lifts R² from 0.08 to 0.42.

Pattern 2: Both Channels are Independent

When both predictors enter jointly (Model 4), VD retains independent significance (t = 5.27), indicating that the two channels capture genuinely different aspects of the data.

Pattern 3: Complementary Interpretation

Variance drain identifies which assets benefit from volatility targeting (those where the compounding penalty on the long investor is largest), while the leverage effect provides the timing signal that makes real-time implementation profitable. The two are complementary: variance drain is the reason timing matters for compounded wealth; the leverage effect is the mechanism through which timing is executed.

7.4 The Cross-Section is Underpowered

For completeness, we also estimate a pure cross-sectional regression using 21 asset-level averages. As one might expect with just 21 data points, the result is noisy: β̂ = 0.26 (R² = 0.04; p = 0.25). This underscores why the panel design, which exploits within-asset time-series variation, is essential for detecting the variance-drain effect with reasonable power.

7.5 Is Variance Drain Just Relabeled Variance?

A natural concern is whether the variance drain predictor VD_{i,t} = SR_0 · cv(σ)² is merely a repackaging of variance itself. We argue it is not, on three grounds:

1

Variance Drain Captures Variability, Not Level

An asset can have high average variance but stable variance over time (cv(σ) ≈ 0), in which case there is nothing to time and no variance drain can be reduced through scaling. Conversely, an asset with moderate average variance but highly time-varying volatility offers substantial scope for timing. The two objects ; level of variance and variability of variance ; are conceptually and empirically distinct.

2

The SR_0 Interaction is Economically Meaningful

An asset with high cv(σ) but zero risk premium (SR_0 = 0) gains nothing from volatility targeting, because the investor has no expected return to protect from the compounding penalty. The product SR_0 · cv(σ)² is thus economically meaningful in a way that variance alone is not.

3

The Joint Regression Provides Direct Empirical Test

The leverage effect predictor ρ_{r,σ} already captures the first-order relationship between returns and variance (it is, by construction, the correlation between returns and subsequent volatility changes). If VD were merely relabeled variance, the leverage effect would subsume it in a multivariate regression. Instead, VD retains independent significance with t = 5.27 even after the leverage effect enters with t = −23.14 (Model 4). The two predictors are capturing genuinely orthogonal dimensions of the data.

08 The Financialization Structural Break

8.1 Methodology

Our century-long sample is long enough to ask a question that shorter datasets cannot address: has the effectiveness of vol-targeting changed over time?

We compute ΔSR within each non-overlapping decade (1930s through 2020s). For a candidate break year τ, we form pre-τ and post-τ subsamples and compute a Chow-type F-statistic:

$$F(\tau) = \frac{(SSR_R - SSR_U)/q}{SSR_U/(n - 2q)}$$

where SSR_R is the sum of squared residuals under the null of no break (pooled model), SSR_U = SSR_1(τ) + SSR_2(τ) is the unrestricted sum from separate pre- and post-τ regressions, q is the number of restrictions, and n is the total number of observations.

We search over candidate break years τ ∈ {1940, 1950, ..., 2010} and select:

$$\tau^* = \arg\max_{\tau} F(\tau)$$

This is a supremum Wald test for an unknown break date (Andrews, 1993). The two-sample t-statistic for the mean ΔSR uses the Welch (1947) approximation for unequal variances.

8.2 Results

The endogenous break year is τ* = 1985, with a supremum F-statistic of 3.22. The estimated pre- and post-break means are:

Pre-1985
ΔSR = −0.047
cv(σ) = 0.44
Volatility scaling was HARMFUL
Post-1985
ΔSR = +0.058
cv(σ) = 0.52
Volatility scaling was BENEFICIAL

Statistical Significance

The difference in ΔSR is statistically significant (Welch t-test, p = 0.014), as is the difference in cv(σ). The total swing in the vol-scaling benefit is 0.058 − (−0.047) = 0.105 Sharpe ratio units.

8.3 Interpretation: The Financialization Effect

What happened around 1985? A constellation of institutional changes reshaped the way financial markets process information:

Mid-1980s

Portfolio insurance rose and fell. Banking and brokerage deregulation accelerated in both the US and UK (Schwert, 1989).

1990s-2000s

Options and futures markets expanded rapidly. Algorithmic trading began to amplify the feedback loop between returns and volatility.

2004

VIX futures introduced, making volatility a directly tradeable asset class.

2006

Leveraged ETFs launched, further amplifying the returns-volatility feedback mechanism.

The net effect, in our framework, was to raise cv(σ) ; the coefficient of variation of volatility ; which theory predicts should strengthen the case for vol-targeting. Before financialization, volatility was too stable (cv(σ) = 0.44) for scaling to help; afterward, it became variable enough (cv(σ) = 0.52) for the strategy to work.

Resolution of the Cederburg et al. (2020) Critique

This structural break offers a resolution to the Cederburg et al. (2020) critique. Studies that pool the entire 1926-2025 record mechanically average the post-1985 benefit with a pre-financialization era in which vol-scaling was actively harmful (ΔSR < 0). Conditioning on era eliminates much of the attenuation that troubled earlier full-sample analyses.

IS = 1926-1975; OOS = 1975-2025. At every level of risk aversion, the CER improvement remains positive out of sample ; a result at odds with the Cederburg et al. (2020) conclusion.

9.2 Response to Barroso and Detzel (2021): Transaction Costs

Barroso and Detzel (2021) rightly emphasized that transaction costs can eat into the gains from frequent rebalancing. For our strategy, the breakeven cost works out to 26bps per unit of turnover over the full 100-year sample.

Safety Margin

Given that typical equity spreads have ranged between 5-10bps since the early 1990s, we estimate a safety margin of at least 16bps ; enough to survive realistic frictions, though admittedly tighter for less liquid instruments.

9.3 Response to Liu et al. (2019): Regime Dependence and Simpson's Paradox

Liu et al. (2019) argued that the benefits of vol-targeting concentrate in high-volatility regimes, raising the question of whether the strategy is truly unconditionally beneficial. We examine this claim by sorting days into realized volatility terciles (using realized vol rather than VIX, which is unavailable before 1990):

Simpson's Paradox

The result is striking: within each tercile, vol-targeting reduces the Sharpe ratio ; yet the unconditional ΔSR is a solidly positive +0.21. This is a textbook instance of Simpson's paradox. The gains come not from improving performance within any regime, but from reallocating exposure across regimes over time. The portfolio overweights calm, high-SR regimes and underweights turbulent, low-SR regimes.

10 Conclusion

This paper has examined volatility-managed portfolios through the lens of the longest daily sample available: 100 years of CRSP returns. The theoretical derivation ΔSR ∝ SR_0 × cv(σ)² lines up reasonably well with the century-long data, reinforcing the idea that vol-targeting works best when an asset has both a worthwhile risk premium and meaningfully time-varying volatility.

Our VDOV allocation rule, tested under a strict walk-forward protocol, achieves an out-of-sample Sharpe ratio of 1.01 ; above the Moreira and Muir (2017) benchmark (0.94), above buy-and-hold (0.79), and, unusually, above its own in-sample estimate (0.89). Internationally, VDOV beats both alternatives in four of five Kenneth French regional markets (80%).

Perhaps the most important finding is the structural break around 1985. The fact that vol-targeting was actively counterproductive before the financialization era (ΔSR = −0.047), and became reliably beneficial afterward (ΔSR = +0.058), goes some way toward reconciling the optimism of Moreira and Muir (2017) with the skepticism of Cederburg et al. (2020): the two groups are, in a sense, studying different regimes.

Our panel regressions add nuance by showing that both the variance drain channel (t = 7.09) and the leverage effect (t = 18.60) contribute independently, with the latter doing the heavier lifting. Crucially, variance drain operates as a portfolio-level compounding penalty on the long investor ; not as a market friction that sustains mispricing. Because the compounding penalty flips sign for short positions, it cannot explain the persistence of overpricing on the short side (Stambaugh et al., 2015). Instead, variance drain provides the economic motive for volatility management (reducing the ½σ² drag on geometric returns), while the leverage effect provides the timing mechanism (negative return-volatility correlation makes real-time scaling feasible).

Together, these results suggest that volatility targeting is not a statistical curiosity but a robust empirical regularity, rooted in the economic logic of compounding-penalty reduction and the well-documented asymmetry between returns and subsequent volatility (Black, 1976; French et al., 1987).
